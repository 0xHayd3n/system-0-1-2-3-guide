# Time, memory, and canalization

This module follows the philosophical argument in §§2.4 and 4 of **arXiv:2503.06138v3**, dated 16 July 2025. It explains the authors' extended interpretation of Henri Bergson; it does not establish that the framework is a measured law of cognition. All page links use one-based PDF pages.

## Why Bergson enters the argument

The authors use Bergson to connect bodily activity, individual thinking, and collective adaptation through their different temporal organizations. Their starting point is **duration**: an event or interaction has a characteristic unfolding that cannot simply be replaced by an arbitrary clock interval. A chemical process takes time to settle; an organism likewise needs time to integrate perception and act. This motivates attention to the timescales intrinsic to the interacting system. The paper presents this through contemporary multi-timescale scholarship, rather than claiming that Bergson proposed modern neural architectures. [§2.4, PDF pp. 13–15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=13)

The **window of the present** is an interval over which ongoing experience can hang together. The present is therefore more than an instantaneous sample. The background discussion cites approximate psychological and perceptual intervals from earlier literature, but these are neither new measurements nor universal boundaries separating Systems 0–3. The important conceptual claim is that temporal integration makes certain forms of cognition possible. [§2.4, PDF p. 14](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=14)

## Keep three temporal questions separate

Figure 5 and §4.1 distinguish three axes. Combining them into one ladder loses much of the argument.

| Axis | Question it answers | What it prevents us from conflating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temporal layers within an organism | What kinds of processes coexist around the present window? | Material dynamics, present activity, and memory are not one uniform stream. |
| Formation of an adaptive landscape | How long did the dispositions supporting today's behavior take to form? | Fast action can depend on slowly acquired learning or evolution. |
| Thickness of the present window | How much temporally extended material can participate in current cognition? | A longer integration window differs from a slower learning rate. |

The third axis is not strictly another difference in timescale, as the authors explicitly acknowledge. It concerns the extent of the effective present. Figure 5's memory layer is part of the Bergsonian temporal discussion; readers should not automatically identify every layer number in that figure with a societal System 3 mechanism. [§4.1 and Fig. 5, PDF pp. 22–23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=22)

For example, an immediate practiced response may occur through a thin present window even though learning shaped that response over a long period. System 2 combines a thicker window with learned dispositions. System 3 additionally involves a social landscape and an extended window. These relationships are conceptual correspondences, not calibrated timing thresholds. [§4.1, PDF p. 23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=23)

## Two memory resources

**Motor memory** preserves acquired ways of proceeding: skills and habits make familiar activity manageable. **Pure memory** preserves particular experiences as resources that need not already be organized around a current practical use. Learning to ride a bicycle changes how one rides; remembering a particular attempt retains a specific episode. The authors relate these categories to procedural and episodic memory, while stressing that Bergson's distinction has a broader scope. [§4.2, PDF p. 23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=23)

Automatic and attentive recognition both use motor memory. Their difference is that attentive recognition can recruit a richer temporal context, reconstruct an object as an image, and sometimes recall a personal scene. A thicker present creates room to use experience in ways that established routines did not anticipate. Thus habit and flexible recollection are complementary resources. If every experience were reduced to a routine, little surplus would remain for improvisation. [§4.2, PDF pp. 23–24](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=23)

The paper compares pure memory with some external-memory mechanisms in machine learning. This is an analogy about access to retained resources. It does not demonstrate that a memory-augmented model possesses Bergsonian memory or subjective temporal experience. Likewise, the comparison with James, Husserl, and contemporary temporal-experience models provides philosophical context, not an experimental adjudication between those traditions. [§4.2, PDF pp. 23–24](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=23)

## Canalization: preparing possibilities before the moment of action

Section 4.3 extends motor memory into **prior optimization**: processes before the current encounter shape which responses will be easy later. Optimization here need not reach a global optimum. A learned skill, inherited morphology, and an environment altered by previous activity can all make some paths more available than others. The relevant boundary is temporal—preparation versus present activity—rather than simply the boundary of the skull. [§4.3, PDF pp. 24–25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=24)

The **canalized landscape** expresses this organization of possibilities. Think of a well-used route that makes movement straightforward: the current traveler benefits from structure prepared earlier. In the paper, canalization draws on Bergson and Waddington, and covers both internal dispositions and features of the organism's action environment. The landscape image is explanatory; the paper does not supply one numerical potential function spanning bodies, neural systems, and societies. [§4.3, PDF p. 25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=25)

This framing also allows a mixed view of representation. Short loops can rely heavily on direct sensorimotor coupling. Longer loops may recruit representations and memory. The authors offer this as a way to consider enactment and representation together, with their roles depending on temporal organization. It is not a proof that a given representational architecture is necessary or sufficient. [§4.3, PDF p. 25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=25)

## From bodily dispositions to shared cultural channels

Section 4.4 traces landscape formation across physical regularity, biological evolution, individual learning, and cultural change. Evolution helps shape bodily affordances associated with System 0. Individual learning organizes skills and conceptual tools relevant to Systems 1 and 2. Social interaction stabilizes norms and linguistic practices associated with System 3. A channel both limits alternatives and makes coordinated activity easier. [§4.4, PDF pp. 25–26](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=25)

The authors then introduce the **Extended Window of the Present (EWP)**. Conversation, notes, transactive memory, and other people's cognitive resources can extend what participates in deliberation beyond an individual's immediate biological integration interval. Their claim concerns socially supported access and coordination. It does not require a literal enlargement of a brain's sensory window, nor does it establish a group subject with phenomenal experience. [§4.4, PDF pp. 26–27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=26)
