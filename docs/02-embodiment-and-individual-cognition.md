# Embodiment and individual cognition

This module follows Systems 0, 1, and 2 in **arXiv:2503.06138v3, 16 July 2025**, separating the authors' synthesis from reviewed prior work. Page numbers are one-based PDF pages.

## System 0: what the body contributes

System 0 names adaptive organization arising from physical interaction between a body and its surroundings, before explicit neural computation. The useful contrast is between a response generated by the material arrangement itself and a response that requires a controller to estimate a condition and compute a correction. Real robots can combine both. Calling a component System 0 does not mean its design required no intelligence or that the whole robot contains no sensors, motors, or learned controller. [§2.2, PDF pp.10–11; §3.1, PDF pp.15–16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=10)

The reviewed examples show how morphology changes control:

| Prior-work example reviewed in the paper | Contribution of the physical system | What the example does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passive dynamic walking | Body mechanics, gravity, and slope can sustain walking without an actuator or computer | General locomotion across arbitrary terrain |
| Soft grasping | Compliance lets fingers conform to differently shaped objects under a common control signal | A universally successful grasping policy |
| Peg insertion with a soft wrist | Contact-driven deformation compensates for pose mismatch while a controller supplies simpler commands | Absence of learned control in the complete task |
| Elastic hopping | Elastic elements help stabilize a repeated motion | A proof that all stability comes from morphology |
| Human muscle preflex | Mechanical response can precede a neural reflex | An equivalence between every biological reflex and passive mechanics |

These examples are discussed as background, chiefly in [§2.2 and Figure 2, PDF pp.10–11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=10). They support the plausibility of giving physical interaction its own explanatory role. The quad-process paper does not rerun these studies or compare a complete four-system robot against a baseline.

### Physical dynamics can also support perception

Physical reservoir computing uses a material system's evolving state as a resource for processing temporal input. In the examples reviewed here, a soft arm's motion carries information about an object or surrounding flow; bending sensors on flexible wings provide patterns informative about wind direction; and finger deformation carries information about a grasped shape. A readout can learn to interpret those states. The physical transformation of the input and the learned interpretation are distinct contributions. [§2.2, PDF p.11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=11)

**Companion illustration:** imagine a compliant gripper whose fingers settle around an unfamiliar object. Their deformation reduces the range of corrections a controller must calculate, while sensor readings may help identify the contact. This is a useful way to picture cooperation between System 0 and System 1; it is not an additional experiment reported by the paper.

## Systems 1 and 2: several routes to fast and slow

In the authors' framing, System 1 involves relatively automatic perception and action, while System 2 involves relatively deliberative organization. The paper follows conventional dual-process definitions, then connects them to AI and robotics. These connections are explanatory correspondences rather than a diagnostic test for locating a system in any given implementation. [§3.1, PDF p.16; §3.3, PDF pp.20–21](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=20)

One route combines learned representations with symbolic manipulation. Section 2.1.1 discusses disentanglement because reasoning about a factor becomes easier when that factor can be varied independently. Attention and modular recurrent mechanisms offer ways to activate a limited set of relevant concepts. Learned program primitives illustrate abstraction followed by reuse. The section also stresses that abstraction alone is insufficient: applying knowledge in a new setting requires deciding what transfers and what should be discarded. [§2.1.1, PDF pp.5–6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=5)

The LLM examples add repeated revision, branching deliberation, tool interaction, and memory to a generative model's immediate response. The paper reads these as System 2-like behavior. Preserve that qualification: an analogy between an engineered workflow and human deliberation is not proof of shared phenomenology, reliability, or internal mechanism. [§2.1.1, PDF pp.6–8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=6)

## Neurodynamics: hierarchy without hand-coded symbolic levels

Section 2.1.2 reviews a constructive tradition in which recurrent dynamics and sensorimotor experience produce a functional hierarchy. A lower level can capture rapid movement primitives, while a higher level captures slower combinations of those primitives. Predictions influence lower-level activity; mismatch with experience sends information upward. Figure 1 visualizes the relationship between predictions, errors, primitives, and their combinations. [§2.1.2, Figure 1, PDF pp.8–9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=8)

Two reviewed architectures implement temporal differences in different ways:

- **RNN with parametric bias (RNNPB):** the paper describes different update frequencies across levels. A higher-level bias conditions lower-level sensorimotor predictions, and prediction errors support adjustment.
- **Multiple timescale RNN (MTRNN):** different time constants produce different rates of state change. Faster states can organize primitives while slower states organize sequences or combinations.

Do not collapse update frequency and time constant into one implementation detail. They are different ways to create temporal structure. The reviewed work also distinguishes lower-level attractor-like organization from higher-level transient or chaotic trajectories. These are accounts of particular models, not a claim that all System 1 activity is periodic or all System 2 activity is chaotic. [§2.1.2, PDF pp.8–9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=8)

The source then discusses stochastic extensions: uncertainty can enter sensorimotor prediction or higher latent organization, with S-MTRNN, PV-RNN, and MTRSSM as examples. These connect the neurodynamical discussion to predictive coding and free-energy approaches. The computational psychiatry examples are cited modeling applications; they should not be read as clinical validation or diagnoses supplied by this framework. [§2.1.2, PDF pp.9–10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=9)

## Representation, experience, and temporal interpretation

Table 1 associates System 0 with physical dynamics, System 1 with implicit representations and world models, and System 2 with explicit internal representations and language models. Section 3.3 explains that explicit internal representation need not equal language. It also distinguishes basic individual interaction from the more personally meaningful interaction associated with System 2. These classifications organize a perspective; they are not measured, mutually exclusive biological compartments. [§3.3, Table 1, PDF pp.20–21](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=20)

The consciousness qualification is especially important. The table's subconscious/conscious contrast concerns **conscious information processing**. The accompanying v3 text explicitly distinguishes that issue from subjective experience, proposing that System 1 contributes foundational sensory qualities while System 2 integrates and reflects on them across a broader duration. Thus, “only System 2 can have experience” misstates the paper. [§3.3, PDF p.21](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=21)

Bergson's automatic and attentive recognition provide another interpretation of fast/slow organization. Both can draw on established motor organization; attentive recognition gains access to a richer resource of particular memories. The authors' later treatment of canalization distinguishes the rapid **use** of an organization from the slower history that **formed** it. A bodily response can be fast while the morphology supporting it developed over evolutionary time. [§4.1–4.3, PDF pp.22–25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06138v3#page=22)

## Questions for a careful reader

1. For each physical example, which task contribution happens in the material and which in a learned readout or controller?
2. How could a study distinguish benefits of temporal hierarchy from benefits of additional model capacity?
3. Does classifying a language model as System 2 specify its update dynamics, or only a role it plays in an explanation?
4. What would need to be measured before turning “super-fast,” “fast,” and “slow” into operational categories?
